Most estate planning attorneys don't think of their practice as paper-heavy. But walk through a typical week and count the moments: the intake forms printed and handed to a new client, the draft mailed for review, the signed copy scanned and emailed back, the executed originals stored in a physical file.

Paper isn't the product. But it's woven into every step of the process — and that has a real cost.

## Time is the most obvious cost

A staff member who spends thirty minutes a day printing, scanning, mailing, and organizing documents spends roughly two and a half hours a week on tasks that don't move work forward. Over a year, that's more than a hundred hours.

Multiply that by the size of your team, and the number gets uncomfortable.

Digital workflows don't eliminate administrative work, but they do eliminate the specific kind of low-value administrative work that paper creates. When a client submits information through a digital intake form, it's already structured, searchable, and ready to use. When a document is signed digitally, there's no scan, no email, no filing — the signed version exists where you need it.

## Errors compound in paper-based processes

Paper creates handoff moments, and every handoff is an opportunity for something to go wrong.

The signature on the wrong line. The page that came through the fax at an angle. The version that got printed before the last round of changes. These aren't catastrophic errors, but they add up — in rework, in follow-up calls, in the occasional matter that has to be redone.

Digital workflows with version control and structured signing flows reduce these errors structurally. The client signs the right document at the right time in the right place, and the record is immutable.

## The client experience gap is real

There's a generational shift underway in who estate planning clients are. Many of your clients today are in their fifties and sixties — people who do their banking online, book travel on their phone, and manage their investments through an app. They're comfortable with technology. They expect it.

When they encounter a law firm that asks them to print, sign, scan, and email documents back — or worse, to drive across town to sign in person — the experience creates friction. It doesn't necessarily cost you the client, but it shapes how they talk about you to their peers.

> "The whole thing was so easy. I signed everything from my kitchen table and everything was just there."

That's the sentence you want clients saying after working with your firm. It's not about being flashy. It's about removing obstacles.

## What "going digital" actually means

A fully digital estate planning workflow looks something like this:

- **Intake** happens through a secure online form — no paper, no email attachments
- **Drafting** is done from structured client data, reducing manual transcription
- **Review** happens asynchronously — clients read their documents and leave questions through a portal
- **Signing** is a structured digital ceremony with witnesses and a notary, conducted over video
- **Storage** is handled automatically — executed documents go to a secure location the family can access

Not every firm needs every piece of this at once. But the firms moving in this direction are discovering that each step compounds: the time saved on intake means more time for drafting, and the time saved on signing means more time for client relationships.

## Where to start

If you're not sure where to begin, start with the signing ceremony. It's often the most time-intensive part of the process for your staff and the most inconvenient part for clients.

Running a single digital signing ceremony — even just to see how it goes — will give you more information than any amount of research. You'll know within an hour whether it's something you want to build into your practice.

The paper isn't going anywhere overnight. But every step away from it is a step toward a more efficient, more scalable, and more client-friendly practice.
